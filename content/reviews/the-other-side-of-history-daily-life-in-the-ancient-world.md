**History for the 10%**

The promise of the premise is what I want from ancient history: the anonymous many instead of kings and battles. You are a Greek soldier the night before battle, you are an Egyptian woman dressing for a party, are among the most interesting exercises history can attempt. But availability bias ascends the slippery slope upward. The sources are written by and about people wealthy enough to be recorded, and Garland follows them there: dinner parties, weddings, respectable households.  This is not the 0.1% history,  it is history for the 1% and 10%.

The frustrating part is that the gap is visible from inside the book. There is a genuinely interesting discussion to be had about comparing modern concepts to  'class' in the ancient world.  Garland misses it. I was wishing for a tighter focus, even if that meant leaning on archaeology and open speculation; a careful "we don't know, but the refuse pits suggest" beats another banquet anecdote. This does not stand up alongside Mary Beard's memoir, whose handling of evidence makes the sourcing here feel thin. A few of the comments on Islam suggest the grasp of anything beyond the classical Mediterranean is modest. And the research production values are podcast-grade. 

Too much upper-class bread, anecdote, and history trope, not enough sufl.
